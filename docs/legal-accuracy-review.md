# Legal accuracy review of the privacy policy, terms and contact page (2026-11-23)

**What this is.** A check of every factual statement in the privacy policy, the terms of use and the contact page (Ukrainian, English, Norwegian) against the real
code and schema and against primary sources found on the web. **What it is not:** legal advice, a lawyer's review, or a statement that the service is "compliant"
with anything. No lawyer was hired (the service is a free, non-commercial hobby project run by a private individual in Ukraine; users may come from the EU/EEA and
Norway). The texts now say so themselves, in a notice at the top of both pages.

**Method.** (1) An **independent reviewer** (a separate agent with a fresh context that saw only the repository, not the author's reasoning, and could not change anything)
checked 64 statements and gaps, read the sources it could reach, and labelled every finding **V** (verified from a source, with URL, article/section and the date read:
2026-11-23), **I** (inferred, from named facts) or **U** (unknown, with the reason). Its complete output is the appendix below, unedited except that a local folder name
was replaced. (2) The **author** then applied the fixes to all three languages, and read the Discogs terms in a normal HTTP client, which the reviewer could not (HTTP 403 for
its client): see "Second pass: Discogs" below. The legal articles of the GDPR were read in an **unofficial reproduction (gdpr-info.eu)**, because EUR-Lex returned an
empty challenge page; where a finding says "V" for an article's wording, it means "read in that reproduction of the official text".

## Result in plain words

* **Statements and gaps checked: 64** (24 in the privacy policy, 13 in the terms, 8 on the contact page, attribution and the documents, 19 gaps: things the code does or a rule asks for that the texts did not say).
* **Mismatches found: 26** (9 clear, 17 partial: supported by the code but incomplete or imprecise; 2 of the 26 were inconsistencies inside the repository's own documents). **All 26 are fixed or answered** below; none was left silent.
* **The 15 `[NEEDS LEGAL REVIEW]` marks** (per language): **9 no longer carry the mark** (8 sentences that now state only what the code and the cited sources support, and the banner, which became the always-shown notice), **6 remain** (each narrowed to the one question that
  could not be answered from sources). The inline mark was removed only where the reviewer found the sentence accurate against code and a cited source.
* **Sources that could not be fetched:** about nine groups (appendix, section 6): EUR-Lex (all of it), CJEU judgments, Brevo's retention article, nkom.no, a Behance page, the Ukrainian bill card, the Latombe appeal docket, Lovdata's current text of § 5.
  Everything that depends on them is marked **U** and stays an open question rather than becoming a claim.

## Not changed on purpose (owner choices, or changes that would weaken or change behaviour)

* **Whether EU/Norwegian data law applies to this service at all** (GDPR Art. 3(2): "offering" a service to people in the EU; EDPB 3/2018 says mere accessibility is not enough but a Norwegian interface and wording that addresses EU/Norwegian users can point to targeting). The sources do not answer yes or no. If it applies, a representative in the EU (Art. 27) is needed unless the processing is "occasional" and unlikely to risk people, which a service that runs all the time can hardly claim; whether anyone would enforce it against a private person in Ukraine is not answered either.
* **The 7-day sign-in cookie and the automatic language cookie** (above): the code is unchanged. Shortening a login or adding a "remember me" box is a product decision about sign-in, not a text fix.
* **The minimum age** and **the Resend/Brevo question** (the code can send through Resend; the text names Brevo; production uses Brevo according to the docs, which the reviewer could not confirm): owner confirmations.
* **Brevo's retention of delivery logs**: unknown (the Brevo article returned 403). The text says "under its own settings" rather than a period. Setting a retention rule in Brevo (Settings > Transactional > Retention rules; click path unverified) would let a period be stated.
* **A personal-data breach procedure** (GDPR Art. 33: 72 hours to the authority when it applies): none exists in the repository. Not a text item; listed as a residual risk below.
* **The stored picture address** (`user.image`) is kept and exported but never shown (data minimisation): the owner could stop storing it.
* **The Ukrainian Law on Personal Data Protection (2297-VI)** was read on zakon.rada.gov.ua (in force; last amendment listed 4240-IX of 12.02.2025); a replacement bill (No. 8153) was reported to have passed a first reading in November 2024, status after 2025 unknown. Re-check at launch.

## Residual risk, in plain language

1. **Does EU/Norwegian law apply to you?** Nobody can say yes or no from these sources; your intent and the texts decide it. If it applies, hobby projects have no exemption when a public service takes sign-ups from strangers.
2. **A nickname as the operator's identity** is untested: the sources require "identity and contact details" and do not say whether a pseudonym is enough. The practical risk is a complaint or a regulator asking who you are.
3. **Cookies:** the 7-day sign-in cookie and the 1-year language cookie are the weakest points against the 2012 EU guidance. The two localStorage flags are probably low risk, but no source clears them.
4. **Providers:** the texts only repeat what the providers say in their own documents. Vercel's DPA is said to cover Pro/Enterprise customers; whether the free Hobby plan gets one is unclear. The EU–US Data Privacy Framework was under appeal at the time of reading (secondary sources); the providers also state the Standard Contractual Clauses.
5. **Brevo may keep a copy of every email** (address, name, sign-in link) beyond the life of the account; until a retention rule is set and stated, the text cannot say that deletion is complete.
6. **Children:** there is no age check; "not designed for children" is a statement of intent, not a control.
7. **Breaches:** if personal data leaks, a report to the authority within 72 hours is expected where the law applies; there is no written procedure.
8. **Liability and governing law** are left open rather than filled with invented text.
9. **The fonts:** the credit now carries author and licence reference, but the font files' own licence string says "Freeware ... NoDerivates" while the design system's notes say CC BY-ND 4.0, and the Ermilov file says "All rights reserved". Which text governs is unknown; the owner has said Lis is their own project and the credit is placed correctly.
10. **The texts stay true only while the facts do:** retention depends on the plans (Vercel, Neon), on the daily purge running, and on environment values nobody but the owner can read (operator name and country, contact email, HSTS).
11. **Dated sources:** the countries' ages of consent (a 2020 snapshot), the Ukrainian bill, a Norwegian proposal to raise the age to 15, and the DPF appeal are moving; re-check at launch.

**M3 privacy.s5P2 (N)** asks whether the cookie/local-storage approach satisfies the rules. No source read supports a statement of compliance, so the mark text cannot become a "yes". What the sources say:

Para 44: information that stays inside the device and is not accessed by anyone else does not constitute "gaining access" (this is about access; storage by the site's script remains within the scope, para 30). The Guidelines "do not address the circumstances under which a processing operation may fall within the exemptions" (para 4, 56). V.
- Exemption B (WP194, quoting Directive 2002/58/EC Art 5(3)): "strictly necessary in order for the provider of an information society service explicitly requested by the subscriber or user to provide the service". V.
- Authentication cookies: exempt "for the duration of a session"; "Persistent login cookies ... are not exempted under CRITERION B" (3.2, 5). The app's session cookie lasts 7 days: **I: it falls under the persistent-login case**. A "remember me" tick box is the working party's example of how to obtain consent. (G08)
- Language preference cookies: exempt only if explicitly requested by the user and "session (or short term)" unless extra information is given prominently, "e.g. 'uses cookies' written next to the flag" (3.6). NEXT_LOCALE lasts 1 year and is also set automatically (G09). I.

### 5. Residual risk (plain language, for a non-lawyer owner)

1. **Does EU/Norwegian data law apply to you at all?** The sources say it applies to a service outside the EU if it is "offered" to people in the EU/EEA, and that a website merely being reachable is not enough, but a Norwegian interface and wording that addresses users "in the EU or Norway" can be read as an offer. Nobody can tell you yes or no from these sources; the texts and your intent decide it. If it applies, the duties in section 3 apply to a hobby service the same as to a company; there is no exemption for hobby projects found for a public service open to strangers.
2. **A representative in the EU (Art 27)** is required if the law applies unless the processing is "occasional" and unlikely to risk people. A service that runs all the time is hard to call "occasional". Whether anyone would enforce this against a private person in Ukraine is not something the sources answer.
3. **Cookies**: the 7-day sign-in cookie and the 1-year language cookie are the weakest points against the 2012 EU guidance; the two localStorage flags are probably low risk but no source clears them. The Norwegian rule is stricter in wording only in that it names the same two exceptions; Nkom decides.
4. **Providers**: the privacy text should not promise more than the providers' own pages. Vercel's DPA is said to apply to Pro/Enterprise customers; whether your free plan gets one is unclear. The EU-US transfer framework is under appeal; if it falls the providers say they also use the standard contractual clauses.
5. **Brevo may keep copies of every email** (address, name, sign-in link) beyond the life of the account; set a retention rule in Brevo and state it, until then the text cannot say deletion is complete.
6. **Pseudonymous operator**: no source says a nickname is or is not enough as the "identity" of the controller; an email address alone is the minimum the sources require for contact; the risk is a complaint or a regulator asking for the real identity.
7. **Children**: the app has no age check; a "not for under 16" sentence is a statement of policy, not a control. If you cannot check ages the sources still require "reasonable efforts" only where consent is the basis.
8. **Breach**: if personal data leaks, the law (if it applies) expects a report to the authority within 72 hours; you have no written procedure.
9. **Discogs, KyivType, Ermilov**: I could not read the Discogs terms (blocked); the credit for KyivType is incomplete as read; the font files' own licence text differs from "CC BY-ND 4.0" and Ermilov's file says "All rights reserved".
10. **Google sign-in creates accounts without any terms/privacy notice** (T01).
11. **Terms limits of liability and governing law** cannot be filled from sources; leaving them out or neutral is safer than invented text.
12. **The texts only help if they stay true**: the retention periods depend on the Vercel/Neon plans (changing a plan changes the statement), on the cron job running daily, and on env settings I could not read (operator name/country/contact, Brevo retention, HSTS).
13. **Dated sources**: the country ages of consent (2020 snapshot), the Ukrainian replacement bill (first reading Nov 2024), the Norwegian age-15 proposal and the DPF appeal are moving targets; re-check at launch.
